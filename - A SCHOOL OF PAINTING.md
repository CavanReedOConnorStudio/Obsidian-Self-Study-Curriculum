# Preface — The Philosophy

Every subject in this handbook exists to answer one question: why did [[Artists.base]] make the work they made?

This handbook is not a study plan in the ordinary sense. It is the outline of a private liberal arts education — your own [[School of Painting]] — in which every subject is chosen because it changes how you see, and therefore how you paint. A study plan lists tasks to complete. An education changes the mind doing the looking.

Painting has never existed in isolation. A panel by [[Giotto]] cannot be separated from the [[Theology]] of [[Assisi]]. A canvas by [[Turner]] cannot be separated from the [[geology]] and [[weather]] of the [[English coast]]. A print by [[Hokusai]] reached [[Paris]] and rearranged the eye of every [[Impressionist]] who saw it. To understand painting is to understand the world that produced it — its beliefs, its books, its politics, its landscapes, its sciences, and its gods.

This handbook therefore treats [[Art History]] as the spine of the course — the Major, occupying the majority of your attention — and surrounds it with nine Minors: [[Philosophy]], [[Literature]], [[History]], [[Geography]], [[Ecology]], [[Biology]] and [[Anatomy]], [[Theology]] [[Astrology]], and [[Language]]. None of these is decorative. Each answers a specific question a painter eventually asks.


> *We do not see with the eye alone, but with the whole apparatus of the mind behind it.*
> 
> *— after [[E.H. Gombrich]]*

A note on scope: this is a Western-centred canon, because that is the tradition most [[European]] and [[American]] painting sits within, and because a coherent education needs a spine before it can grow branches. But the West was never sealed off from the rest of the world.[[ Islamic geometry]] shaped [[Gothic tracery]] and [[Renaissance ornament]]. [[Chinese porcelain]] and [[5 - Tags/painting]] shaped [[eighteenth-century]] taste. [[Japanese prints]] reorganised European composition after 1860. Wherever these currents cross the Western story, this handbook marks them, so that the canon is read as it actually happened — as an open, trading, borrowing tradition — rather than as a closed room.

## How to Use This Handbook

The handbook is organised in four parts.

### PART I — [[THE MAJOR]]: [[THE HISTORY OF ART]]

This is roughly two-thirds of your study. It moves chronologically from the [[ancient world]] to the present, period by period, with the key figures, the ideas at stake, and what to actually look for when you stand in front of a work. A dedicated chapter, East Meets West, gathers the major points of contact between Western and [[Asian art]] into one place.

### PART II — [[THE NINE MINORS]]

Each minor exists to answer a specific question about how artists thought, saw, and worked. Each has a short rationale, a list of core topics, a compact reading list, and one practical exercise that ties the subject back to your own painting.

### PART III — [[THE WESTERN CANON]], AND THE EAST WITHIN IT

A single reading and viewing spine — the books, and a note on the Eastern currents that run through it.

## PART IV — [[STRUCTURE AND RHYTHM]]

A suggested weekly rhythm and a four-year plan for working through the whole handbook without losing momentum.

There is no correct order beyond the chronology of the Major itself. Read a period of art history, then read the minor that illuminates it — [[Theology]] alongside [[Giotto]] [[Astrology]] alongside the [[Renaissance court]], ecology alongside the [[Barbizon painters]], [[Japanese art]] alongside [[Impressionism]]. The subjects are cross-referenced throughout for this reason.

# Part I — The Major: The History of Art

Roughly 60–70% of total study. Everything else in this handbook exists to support this spine.

The Major is arranged chronologically. Each period is treated the same way: the historical situation that produced the work, the artists who defined it, and — most importantly — what to actually look for when standing in front of a painting from that period. Read each chapter alongside the minor subjects flagged within it; art history alone explains what changed, but [[Philosophy]], [[Theology]], [[Geography]] and the rest explain why it changed.

## On How to Look at a Painting

Before working through the chronology, it is worth fixing a method for looking, since the same six questions apply to every period below and turn passive viewing into active study.

• [[Function]]: what was this object for — [[worship]], [[memory]], [[persuasion]], [[decoration]], sale?

• [[Patron]]: who paid for it, and what did they want it to say about them?

• [[Materials]]: [[fresco]], [[tempera]], [[oil]], [[panel]], [[canvas]] — each has a history and a set of constraints

• [[Composition]]: where does the eye enter, and where is it led?

• [[Light]]: is it describing a real time of day, or serving a [[symbolic]] or [[dramatic]] purpose?

• [[Precedent]]: what earlier work is this painting answering, copying, or rejecting?

Return to this list throughout Part I. Applied consistently, it turns the difference between periods into a set of specific, comparable answers rather than a vague sense of 'style.'

## 1. [[The Ancient World]]

c. 3000 BCE – 400 CE — [[Egypt]], [[Greece]], [[Rome]]

[[Egyptian art]] fixed a formula — [[frontal figures]], [[hierarchical scale]], [[profile faces]] — that lasted three thousand years because it served [[religion]], not [[observation]]: it recorded the [[eternal]], not the momentary. [[Greek art]] broke from this in the opposite direction, chasing [[naturalism]], [[proportion]], and the idealised [[human body]] as a [[civic]] and [[philosophical]] statement. [[Rome]] absorbed [[Greek]] models wholesale but added [[portraiture]] of startling individual likeness, and the [[illusionistic wall painting]] later rediscovered at [[Pompeii]].

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Egyptian tomb painters]] (anonymous); [[Polykleitos]] and [[the Canon of proportion]]; [[Apelles]] (by reputation only); [[the Pompeian fresco painters]] of the Second and Fourth Styles.

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

The relationship between a picture's formula and its purpose. Ask what a work was for — [[worship]], [[memory]], [[status]], [[decoration]] — before asking whether it looks 'real'. Read alongside Minor VII, [[Theology]], and Minor IX, [[Astrology]], for the cosmology behind [[Egyptian]] and [[Greco-Roman]] imagery.

## 2. [[Byzantium and the Medieval World]]

c. 400 – 1300 CE

With the decline of [[Rome]], the image became almost entirely a vehicle for doctrine. [[Byzantine]] [[icons]] used [[gold]] ground, [[reverse perspective]], and fixed types to represent [[eternity]] rather than a moment in time — the picture was a window onto the sacred, not onto the world. In the [[Latin]] West, [[manuscript illumination]] and [[Romanesque]] and [[Gothic]] [[church]] [[sculpture]] carried the same weight, teaching [[scripture]] to a largely illiterate population in glass and stone.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[The Hagia Sophia mosaicists]]; the [[Andrei Rublev]] workshop tradition (slightly later, Russian); the illuminators of [[the Book of Kells]] and [[the Lindisfarne Gospels]]; the sculptors of [[Chartres Cathedral.]]

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Gold as light rather than decoration, [[inverse perspective]] as [[Theology]] rather than error, and the total absence of a 'wrong' angle — every element is [[symbolic]]. Essential reading alongside Minor VII, Theology.

## 3. [[The Early Renaissance]]

c. 1300 – 1450 — [[Florence]], Siena

[[Giotto]] broke the [[Byzantine]] formula by giving figures [[weight]], [[emotion]], and a believable [[space]] to occupy — [[the Arena Chapel]] is the hinge on which a thousand years of art turns. A century later, [[Brunelleschi]]'s discovery of mathematical [[linear perspective]] gave [[Florentine painters]] a system for constructing a rational, measurable world on a flat surface, and [[humanism]] gave them a reason to want one.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Giotto]] di Bondone; [[Masaccio]]; [[Fra Angelico]]; [[Piero della Francesca]]; [[Sandro Botticelli]]; the sculptor-theorists [[Ghiberti]] and [[Donatello]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

[[The vanishing point]], the modelling of drapery to suggest a body beneath it, and the new interest in individual, recognisable human faces even in religious scenes. Read alongside Minor IV, Geography — why [[Florence]], specifically, produced this — and Minor I, Philosophy, for humanism.

## 4. [[The High Renaissance]]

c. 1450 – 1520 — [[Florence]], [[Rome]], [[Venice]]

Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael brought [[The Early Renaissance]] experiments to a [[classical synthesis]]: [[anatomical precision]], [[atmospheric perspective]], and [[idealised composition]] balanced against individual [[psychological]] presence. In [[Venice]], a parallel tradition under [[Bellini]], Giorgione and Titian prized [[colour]] and [[Light]]— [[colorito]] — over the Florentine emphasis on [[line]] and [[drawing]] — [[disegno]], a debate that would echo for three centuries.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Leonardo da Vinci]]; [[Michelangelo]] [[Buonarroti]]; [[Raphael]] Sanzio; [[Giorgione]]; [[Titian]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

[[Sfumato]] and the disappearance of hard outlines in Leonardo; the muscular, sculptural bodies of Michelangelo even in paint; the [[disegno]]–[[colorito]] debate, essential background for every later argument between drawing-led and colour-led painting. Read alongside Minor VI, Biology & Anatomy, and Minor IX, Astrology, for the [[Neoplatonic]] cosmology behind works like [[Botticelli]]'s [[Primavera]].

## 5. [[The Northern Renaissance]]

c. 1400 – 1550 — [[Flanders]], [[Germany]], [[the Netherlands]]

North of the Alps, painters developed oil [[glazing]] techniques of extraordinary precision, producing surfaces of jewel-like detail and [[symbolic]] density — every object in a [[Northern interior]] can carry meaning. Where Italy idealised the body, the North observed the specific: individual faces, textures, and the natural world rendered with near-scientific attention.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Jan van Eyck]]; [[Rogier van der Weyden]]; [[Hieronymus Bosch]]; [[Albrecht Dürer]]; [[Pieter Bruegel the Elder]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Disguised [[symbolism]] — ordinary objects (a dog, a candle, a mirror) carrying [[doctrinal]] or [[moral weight]] — and the layered [[oil glaze]] technique that gives Northern panels their luminous depth. Read alongside Minor V, [[Ecology]], for Bruegel's [[landscapes]] and seasons.

## 6. [[The Baroque]]

c. 1600 – 1720 — [[Italy]], [[Spain]], [[Flanders]], the [[Dutch]] Republic

[[The Counter-Reformation Church]] wanted art that moved the faithful bodily, not just instructed them, producing [[Caravaggio]]'s violent [[chiaroscuro]] and [[Bernini]]'s theatrical [[sculpture]]. In the newly [[Protestant]] and [[mercantile]] Dutch Republic, without church commissions, painting turned toward [[portraiture]], [[still life]], and everyday [[domestic scenes]] — an entirely new market-driven art.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Caravaggio]]; [[Artemisia Gentileschi]]; [[Peter Paul Rubens]]; [[Diego Velázquez]]; [[Rembrandt van Rijn]]; [[Johannes Vermeer]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

[[Tenebrism]] and [[directional light]] as drama; the rise of [[genre painting]] and [[still life]] as subjects worthy of art in their own right, tied directly to a new Protestant, commercial society. Read alongside Minor III, History, for the [[Reformation]] and [[Counter-Reformation]], and Minor VII, [[Theology]]

## 7. [[Rococo and Neoclassicism]]

c. 1720 – 1800 — [[France]]

The Rococo of Watteau, Boucher and Fragonard gave aristocratic France a painting of [[pastel colour]], [[pastoral]] flirtation, and [[decorative]] lightness. The [[French Revolution]] demanded its opposite: Jacques-Louis David's [[Neoclassicism]] revived Roman austerity, clear outline, and moral seriousness as a visual language for republican virtue.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Jean-Antoine Watteau]]; [[François Boucher]]; [[Jean-Honoré Fragonard]]; [[Jacques-Louis David]]; [[Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

How abruptly style can change when political meaning changes — the same brush skill turned from silk and flesh to stone and sacrifice within a single generation. Read alongside Minor III, History, for [[the Revolution]], and note the era's taste for [[Chinoiserie]], covered in the East Meets West chapter below.

## 8. [[Romanticism]]

c. 1780 – 1850 — [[Britain]], [[France]], [[Germany]], [[Spain]]

Against [[Neoclassical]] reason, the [[Romantics]] prized [[emotion]], [[the sublime]], and [[the individual imagination]]— [[nature]] as overwhelming rather than orderly, [[History]] as tragedy rather than lesson. Turner dissolved landscape into light and weather; Friedrich made the solitary human figure small before an immense natural world; Goya turned court painting into nightmare and protest.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Francisco Goya]]; [[J.M.W. Turner]]; [[John Constable]]; [[Caspar David Friedrich]]; [[Eugène Delacroix]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

The sublime — beauty mixed with terror or awe — and the new seriousness given to landscape and weather as subjects in themselves. Read alongside Minor V, [[Ecology]], and Minor IV, [[Geography]], for the specific coastlines, mountains and skies each painter actually studied.

## 9. [[Realism and the Birth of Modern Life]]

c. 1840 – 1870 — [[France]]

[[Realism]] - Courbet insisted on [[5 - Tags/painting]] only what he could see with his own eyes — [[peasants]], labourers, funerals — at the scale and seriousness previously reserved for [[history painting]], a deliberately political act. Millet gave rural labour a monumental dignity; both prepared the ground for Manet's provocations and, eventually, [[Impressionism]].

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Gustave Courbet]]; [[Jean-François Millet]]; [[Honoré Daumier]]; [[Édouard Manet]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Scale as a political statement — a peasant painted as large as a king was once painted — and the rejection of myth and allegory in favour of contemporary, often uncomfortable, subject matter. Read alongside Minor III, History, for 1848 and industrialisation.

## 10. [[Impressionism]] and [[Post-Impressionism]]

c. 1860 – 1900 — [[France]]

The [[Impressionists]] painted outdoors, directly, chasing the changing effects of light with visible, [[broken brushwork]] rather than the smooth finish demanded by the official [[Salon]]. The [[Post-Impressionists]] who followed kept the bright palette but reasserted [[structure]] and personal vision: Cézanne's [[geometry]], Van Gogh's emotional [[colour]], Seurat's [[optical science]], Gauguin's flattened [[symbolic pattern]].

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Claude Monet]]; [[Edgar Degas]]; [[Berthe Morisot]]; [[Paul Cézanne]]; [[Vincent van Gogh]]; [[Georges Seurat]]; [[Paul Gauguin]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Broken colour and optical mixing; the abandonment of a fixed, [[single viewpoint]] in Degas and Cézanne; and, throughout this whole period, the direct influence of [[Japanese prints]] on [[Composition]] and [[flat colour]] — see the [[East Meets West]] chapter, which belongs precisely here.

## 11. [[Modernism]] I — [[Fauvism]], [[Cubism]], [[Expressionism]]

c. 1900 – 1920 — [[France]], [[Germany]]

Matisse and the Fauves pushed [[colour]] beyond description into pure emotional force. Picasso and Braque dismantled single-viewpoint perspective entirely in [[Cubism]], showing an object from several angles at once — the most complete rejection of the system [[Brunelleschi]] had built five centuries earlier. In [[Germany]], the [[Expressionists]] used [[distortion]] and [[raw colour]] to convey [[psychological]] and [[social anxiety]].

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Henri Matisse]]; [[Pablo Picasso]]; [[Georges Braque]]; [[Ernst Ludwig Kirchner]]; [[Wassily Kandinsky]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

The final collapse of illusionistic space as the default goal of painting, and colour used as feeling rather than description. Read alongside Minor I, [[Philosophy]], for [[phenomenology]], and Minor IX, Astrology, for Kandinsky's [[theosophical]] and [[cosmological]] [[theory of colour]].

12. [[Modernism]] II — [[abstraction]], [[Surrealism]], [[Abstract Expressionism]]

c. 1910 – 1960 — [[Europe]], [[United States]]

Kandinsky and Malevich pursued abstraction as a spiritual language freed from the object entirely. [[Surrealism]], led by Dalí and Magritte, mined dream and the [[unconscious]]. After the [[Second World War]], the centre of gravity shifted to [[New York]], where Pollock and Rothko pushed [[abstraction]] toward pure [[gesture]] and pure feeling at an enormous physical scale.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Wassily Kandinsky]]; [[Kazimir Malevich]]; [[Salvador Dalí]]; [[René Magritte]]; [[Jackson Pollock]]; [[Mark Rothko]].

##### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

Scale as [[immersion]], gesture as [[subject matter]], and the search — across very different artists — for an art that bypasses recognisable [[imagery]] altogether. Read alongside Minor I, [[Philosophy]], for [[existentialism]].

## 13. [[Contemporary Art]] and the Return of [[Figuration]]

c. 1960 – present

Since the 1960s, painting has repeatedly been declared dead and repeatedly returned, often through a renewed interest in the [[human figure]], [[personal history]], and [[identity]], alongside continuing [[abstract]] and [[conceptual]] traditions. Painters today work with the entire preceding canon as available material, in a global rather than purely Western conversation.

#### KEY FIGURES

[[Lucian Freud]]; [[Gerhard Richter]]; [[Peter Doig]]; [[Kerry James Marshall]]; [[Njideka Akunyili Crosby]]; [[Jenny Saville]].

#### WHAT TO LOOK FOR

How [[contemporary]] painters quote, rework, and answer earlier periods in this handbook — a Freud nude in dialogue with [[Titian]], a Doig landscape in dialogue with Gauguin and Munch. This is where your own painting joins the conversation.

## 14. [[East Meets West]]: Cross-Currents in the Canon

A smaller but essential chapter — the points where [[Asian art]] reshaped the Western tradition, and vice versa.

The Western canon has never been a closed system, and a handbook that pretends otherwise misrepresents its own subject. Three moments matter most.

[[Islamic Art]] and the [[Medieval-to-Renaissance West]]

[[Islamic geometric ornament]], [[arabesque]], and [[calligraphic design]] entered [[Europe]] through Sicily, [[Spain]], and trade with [[Byzantium]] and the [[Ottoman]] world, shaping [[Gothic tracery]], [[textile patterns]] in paintings by [[Gentile Bellini]] and [[Carlo Crivelli]], and the [[Renaissance]] taste for '[[Turkish carpets]]' visible in [[Northern European portraiture]]. [[Ottoman-Venetian]] contact, including Bellini's own visit to Istanbul, ran directly through this exchange.

[[Chinoiserie]] and the Eighteenth Century

[[Chinese porcelain]], [[lacquer]], and painting reached Europe via trade with the [[Qing dynasty]], producing the [[Rococo]] fashion for chinoiserie — [[pagodas]], [[willow patterns]], and imagined Chinese scenes in Boucher and Fragonard's decorative schemes — a taste for the East that was fantastical rather than accurate, but genuinely influential on [[colour]] and [[Composition]].

[[Japonisme]] and [[Impressionism]]

The opening of [[Japan]] to Western trade in the 1850s flooded Paris with [[ukiyo-e woodblock]] prints. Their flat colour, cropped and [[asymmetrical compositions]], [[high viewpoints]], and outline-driven design directly reshaped Western painting: Monet collected [[Hokusai]] and [[Hiroshige]], Van Gogh copied Hiroshige prints outright, Degas borrowed Japanese cropping for his ballet and racecourse scenes, and [[Whistler]] and [[Klimt]] absorbed Japanese pattern into their own work. This is arguably the single most concentrated transfer of visual ideas from East to West in the whole canon.

Twentieth Century and Beyond

[[Zen]] aesthetics and [[Chinese]] and [[Japanese ink painting]] fed directly into American [[Abstract Expressionism]] through artists like [[Franz Kline]] and [[Mark Tobey]], while contemporary painting is now a genuinely global conversation rather than a one-directional export from Paris or New York.

#### SUGGESTED READING

• [[Hokusai]], Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji — view alongside any Monet series painting

• [[Ernst Gombrich]], The Story of Art, chapters on Islamic and Chinese art

• [[Colta Ives, The Great Wave]]: The Influence of Japanese Woodcuts on French Prints

# Part II — [[THE NINE MINORS]]

Each minor answers one specific question a painter eventually asks. None is optional; each supports the Major directly.

## [[Minor I]] — [[Philosophy]]

Philosophy changes how an artist thinks, not just what they know. Aesthetics asks what beauty is and whether it can be defined at all; phenomenology asks what perception itself actually consists of, which matters enormously to anyone trying to paint what they see rather than what they know is there; existentialism gave twentieth-century painters a vocabulary for anxiety, freedom and authenticity that shows up directly in Abstract Expressionism.

### CORE TOPICS

• [[Ancient Philosophy]]: [[Plato]]'s theory of [[Forms]] and [[mimesis]], [[Aristotle]]'s [[Poetics]]

• [[Logic]]: [[basic argument structure]], enough to read philosophy critically

• [[Ethics]]: [[virtue]], [[duty]], and consequence as they appear in narrative painting

• [[Aesthetics]]: [[beauty]], [[the sublime]], [[taste]], and [[judgment]]

• [[phenomenology]]: [[perception]], [[embodiment]], the difference between seeing and knowing

• [[existentialism]]: [[freedom]], [[anxiety]] [[authenticity]]

• [[Philosophy of Art]]: what a work of art actually is, and what it is for

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[Plato]], [[The Republic]], [[Book X]] (on mimesis)

• [[Aristotle]], [[Poetics]]

• [[Immanuel Kant]], [[Critique of Judgment]] (selections on the sublime)

• [[Edmund Burke]], [[Philosophical Enquiry into the Sublime and Beautiful]]

• [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty]], [[Eye and Mind]]

• [[Jean-Paul Sartre]], [[Being and Nothingness]] (selections)

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Read Merleau-Ponty's 'Eye and Mind' the same week you attempt a still life from direct observation. Note, in writing, every moment where what you paint contradicts what you 'know' the object looks like.

## [[Minor II]] — [[Literature]]

This is not literary analysis for its own sake — it is the question 'what books shaped artists?' Painters have always been readers, and entire movements are unreadable without their literary sources: [[Pre-Raphaelite]] painting without Dante and Malory, [[symbolism]] without Baudelaire, Surrealism without the unconscious explored by Joyce and Woolf.

#### CORE TOPICS

• [[Epic]] and [[myth]] as the raw material of [[history painting]]

• [[The Divine Comedy]] and its afterlife in Blake, Delacroix, Rodin, and the Pre-Raphaelites

• [[Shakespeare]] as a source for centuries of narrative painting

• [[The novel]] and the rise of [[psychological interiority]]

• [[Symbolist]] and [[Decadent poetry]] alongside [[Symbolist]] painting

• [[Modernist]] [[stream-of-consciousness]] alongside [[abstraction]]

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[Homer]], [[The Iliad]] and [[The Odyssey]]

• [[Dante]], [[The Divine Comedy]]

• [[William Shakespeare]], selected plays ([[Hamlet]], [[The Tempest]], [[Macbeth]])

• [[Miguel de Cervantes]], [[Don Quixote]]

• [[Johann Wolfgang von Goethe]], [[Faust]]

• [[Charles Baudelaire]], [[The Flowers of Evil]]

• [[James Joyce]], [[Dubliners]] (start here before [[Ulysses]])

• [[Virginia Woolf]], [[To the Lighthouse]]

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Choose one canvas from this handbook drawn directly from literature (Delacroix's Dante and Virgil, Millais's Ophelia). Read the source passage, then write two hundred words on exactly what the painter chose to include, omit, and invent.

Not dates for their own sake, but the structural forces — [[political change]], [[cities]], [[trade]], [[religion]], [[technology]], [[empire]] — that determined who could [[commission]] art, what subjects were permitted, and which cities became artistic capitals in the first place.

#### CORE TOPICS

• [[The rise and fall of the city-state]] ([[Florence]], [[Venice]], [[Siena]])

• [[Reformation and Counter-Reformation]] as [[image politics]]

• [[Absolute monarchy]] and [[court patronage]]

• [[Revolution]] — [[American]], [[French]], and their [[visual propaganda]]

• [[Empire]] and [[colonial trade]] as an art-market force

• [[Industrialisation]] and [[the birth of the modern city]]

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

For any one painting in Part I, write a one-page 'patronage note': who paid for it, why, what they wanted from it, and what constraints that placed on the artist.

## [[Minor IV]] — [[Geography]]

Landscape shapes civilisation, and civilisation shapes what gets painted. Florence sat on a river trade route with wool money to spend; Venice grew rich on maritime trade with the East and painted in a correspondingly luminous, colour-saturated way; Paris centralised [[patronage]], academies and dealers in one city; Edinburgh's stone, light and [[topography]] produced a specific and recognisable visual culture of its own.

#### CORE TOPICS

• Why [[Florence]]: [[rivers]], [[wool trade]], [[banking]], and [[civic pride]]

• Why [[Venice]]: [[the lagoon]], [[maritime empire]], and [[trade]] with [[Byzantium]] and [[the Islamic world]]

• Why [[Paris]] became the capital of nineteenth-century art: [[centralisation]], [[academies]], [[dealers]]

• Why [[Edinburgh]] looks the way it does: [[geology]], [[Light]], and [[the Enlightenment city]]

• [[Regional light]] and its effect on [[palette]]: [[Provence]], [[the Dutch coast]], [[the Scottish Highlands]]

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[Fernand Braudel]], [[Civilization and Capitalism]] (selections on trade cities)

• [[Peter Ackroyd]], [[Venice- Pure City]]

• [[Jonathan Jones]], [[essays on regional light and landscape painting]]

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Pick a city from this handbook you have visited or can visit. Walk it deliberately, noting the quality of light, the building stone, and the water or hills nearby — then find one painting produced there and identify which of these physical facts appears in it.

## [[Minor V]] — [[Ecology]]

Not [[environmental politics]], but the things painters actually observe: [[trees]], [[rivers]] [[weather]], [[seasons]], [[geology]]. A [[landscape painter]] who cannot identify a [[species of tree]], read a [[cloud formation]], or understand how a [[river valley]] was carved will paint a generic landscape rather than a specific, truthful one.

#### CORE TOPICS

• Tree identification and seasonal change, especially species common in [[European landscape painting]]

• [[Cloud types]] and [[weather systems]], particularly relevant to Constable and Turner

• [[River systems]], [[erosion]], and [[valley formation]]

• Basic [[geology]]: how [[limestone]], [[chalk]], [[granite]] and [[sandstone]] produce different landscapes and different light

• The seasonal cycle as a [[compositional structure]] (Bruegel's Months, Monet's series paintings)

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[John Constable]]'s cloud studies and letters on skying

• [[Richard Mabey]], [[Flora Britannica]] (reference)

• [[Marcia Bjornerud]], [[Reading the Rocks - An Introduction to Geology]]

• [[John Ruskin]], [[Modern Painters]], volume on 'Of Truth' (skies and mountains)

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Spend one hour outdoors with a sketchbook, naming rather than merely drawing: identify the tree species, the cloud type, and the rock or soil underfoot before you make a single mark.

## [[Minor VI ]]— [[Biology & Anatomy]]

[[Anatomy]], [[perception]], and [[botany]] are the sciences painters have always needed most directly. [[Renaissance]] [[figure painting]] depended on [[dissection]]; the study of [[vision]] and [[perception]] explains why certain painted [[illusions]] work on the eye at all; [[botanical accuracy]] underpins everything from [[Northern Renaissance]] symbolism to [[Pre-Raphaelite]] detail.

CORE TOPICS

• Skeletal and muscular anatomy sufficient for figure drawing

• The anatomy of the head, hands, and feet, the hardest and most revealing parts

• [[The physiology of vision]]: how the eye and brain construct colour, depth and motion

• [[Botany]]: [[plant structure]] and [[identification]], especially flowers with symbolic use in painting

• [[Comparative anatomy]] for animal painting

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[Andreas Vesalius]], [[De Humani Corporis Fabrica]] (illustrations)

• [[Leonardo da Vinci]]'s anatomical notebooks (selections)

• [[Bridgman's Complete Guide to Drawing from Life]]

• [[Margaret Livingstone]], [[Vision and Art - The Biology of Seeing]]

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Draw the same hand from life six times in a single sitting, from six different angles, without reference to a photograph — until the underlying bone structure, not the surface skin, becomes what you are actually drawing.

## [[Minor VII]] — [[Theology]]

Possibly the most overlooked subject by artists today, and one of the most necessary. [[Giotto]], [[Fra Angelico]], [[Michelangelo]], [[Raphael]], [[Rembrandt]] and [[El Greco]] cannot be understood without [[Christian doctrine]], and [[Buddhism]], [[Shinto]] and [[Islam]] shaped visual culture just as decisively wherever they took root.

CORE TOPICS

• [[The Christian narrative cycle]]: [[Annunciation]], [[Nativity]], [[Crucifixion]], [[Resurrection]], as recurring compositional problems

• [[Iconography]]: attributes, symbols, and how to 'read' a saint or scene

• [[The Reformation]]'s rejection of religious imagery and its visual consequences

• [[Buddhist]] and [[Shinto]] image traditions and their very different relationship to representation

• [[Islamic aniconism]] and the resulting flowering of [[geometric]] and [[calligraphic art]]

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• [[The Bible]], especially Genesis, the Gospels, and Revelation

## [[Minor IX]] — [[Language]]

Primarily [[Danish]], [[English]] and [[Gaelic]], but the deeper aim is [[etymology]] — understanding why words mean what they do, since so much art vocabulary ([[fresco]], [[chiaroscuro]], [[Sfumato]], [[ukiyo-e]], [[disegno]]) only fully makes sense in its original tongue.

#### CORE TOPICS

• Working reading knowledge of Danish, for [[Nordic art history]] and primary sources

• English, as the primary working language of most secondary art history literature

• Gaelic, for [[Scottish place names]], [[poetry]], and [[visual culture]]

• [[Etymology]] of core [[art-historical vocabulary]]: fresco, tempera, chiaroscuro, sfumato, disegno, ukiyo-e

• Reading period documents (letters, treatises) in something closer to their original register

#### SUGGESTED READING & STUDY

• A structured Danish course (e.g. Assimil or Teach Yourself Danish)

• An [[etymological dictionary]] kept permanently on the desk

• Basic Gaelic place-name guide (useful directly for Minor IV, Geography)

• [[Giorgio Vasari]], [[Lives of the Artists]], read in translation alongside the original Italian terms

#### PRACTICAL EXERCISE

Build a running glossary of twenty core art-historical terms, tracing each word to its root language and original literal meaning, and keep adding to it throughout the whole course.

# Appendix — [[Glossary of Core Terms]]

• [[chiaroscuro]] (Italian, 'light-dark') — strong contrast between light and shadow to model form

• [[Sfumato]] (Italian, 'smoked') — soft, gradual transitions between tones, associated with Leonardo

• [[disegno]] / [[colorito]] — the Renaissance debate between drawing-led (Florence/Rome) and colour-led (Venice) painting

• [[Tenebrism]] — an extreme form of chiaroscuro with dramatic, near-total darkness, associated with Caravaggio

• [[ukiyo-e]] (Japanese, 'pictures of the floating world') — Japanese woodblock prints, central to Japonisme

• [[Iconography]] — the study of symbolic content and subject matter in a work of art

• [[genre painting]] — scenes of everyday life, elevated to serious subject matter in the Dutch Golden Age

• [[the sublime]] — an aesthetic category combining beauty with awe, terror, or vastness

• [[Contrapposto]] — a standing figure posed with weight on one leg, producing a naturalistic S-curve

## Appendix — A Consolidated Checklist

A single running checklist drawn from every exercise in Part II, useful as a self-assessment before moving from one year of the four-year plan to the next.

• Can you name the historical situation and patron behind any five paintings in Part I without looking them up?

• Have you completed at least one iconography reading of an unfamiliar religious painting?

• Have you drawn the same hand, or another body part, from life at least six times in one sitting?

• Have you produced one 'patronage note' connecting a painting to the political and economic forces that produced it?

• Have you spent at least one hour outdoors identifying trees, clouds, and geology before sketching?

• Have you kept and added to a running etymological glossary of art-historical terms?

• Have you compared a Western painting directly against an Asian work it was influenced by, or that influenced it?

• Have you read at least one full primary text from the Literature minor's list, rather than a summary of it?

## Appendix — A Note on This Handbook

This handbook is a beginning, not a finished map. Its purpose is to give a self-directed education in painting the same seriousness of structure that a university degree would — a Major that anchors everything, Minors chosen because they answer real questions, and a canon read honestly, including the currents running into it from outside the West. Treat every reading list here as a floor, not a ceiling, and follow whatever thread in it pulls hardest.
