# Introduction

  

19th Century Dutch nationalism helped shape a canon based on the idea that authentically Dutch art is realist. Until recently art historians often paid little attention to paintings that did not fall under this category. However, the schools of iltalianate and classical painting continued in the Netherlands in the [[17th Century]], as well as the continued art styles of historical and mythological scenes. Dutch [[Mannerism]] is a term to describe a self consciously refined and artficial manner of painting (maniera) whose origins can be traced back to [[16th Century]] Italian art. [[Joachim Wtewael]] (1566-1638) paintings (Noted - [[The Judgement of Paris]] ) can be labelled under this style due to the distinct differences in both subject matter and treatment to those more familiar examples of Dutch Art.

  

Another movement were the [[Caravagisti]], a important school of artists based in [[Utrecht]], were heavily influenced by the style of the Italian painter [[Caravaggio]] (1571-1610). These artists employ dramatic light effects to explore the contrast between areas of bright illumination and deep shadow - [[chiaroscuro]]. [[Henrdrick ter Brugghen]] is a noted artist for his work [[The Concert]]. The exhibition *Gods, Saints and Heroes: Dutch Painting in the Age of Rembrandt* (1981) aided in marginalising the opinion that these different styles of painting were insufficiently "Dutch".

  

## Rembrandt

  

Fromentin was obliged to characterise Rembrandt as "an exception in his own country and everywhere else" (Fromentin, 1948) since it contrasted his claim on Dutch art. Rembrandt had extraordinary range as an artist making categorising him a difficult task. However, the work *[Belshazzar's Feast]* has been re-assessed to fall under a realist interpretation, even though it a re-imagining of a story taken from the Old Testament. The work itself combines a masterful attention to the play of light over different textures and surfaces, set in a religious scene. In recent years Dutch art has opened up the field of study to include the full range of subjects, themes, and types of painting that were made and purchased in the Netherlands. however, the main challenge to the realist interpretation came not from this more inclusive approach to Dutch art, but from the development of an alternative way of understanding the small-scale paintings of domestic scenes and unprepossessing tracts on land on which Hegel and Fromentin had focused their attention.
