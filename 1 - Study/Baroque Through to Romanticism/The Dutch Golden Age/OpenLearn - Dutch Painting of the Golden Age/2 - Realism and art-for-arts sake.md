# Introduction

> *It is evident that the fundamental tendency of this painting is above all **Realistic*** 
> - Wilhelm Bode, 1883

From the late [[19th Century]] to the mid [[20th Century]], it was widely accepted that the [[Dutch Golden Age]] was distinguished from other national traditions by its realism. *[[Realism]]* seems to mean two different but related things.

1. Used to describe the subject matter of everyday life, such as domestic and tavern scenes. The term Realism here is a matter of *what* is being represented
2. Used to characterise a form of painting that purportedly offered a more truthful or exact imitation of visual appearances. The term Realism here is a matter of *how* an object or scene is represented. 
(Bialostocki, 1984)

Although these forms of realism frequently appear together, there is no necessary connection between them; it is possible to create a idealised image of a street scene, as it is possible to create a realistic image of royalty. Because of this double meaning, art historians accommodated a broad spectrum of Dutch paintings under the umbrella [[Realism]]. 

## 'Nothing but painting well'

[[G.W.F. Hegel]]'s view that the increased interest in painting for paintings sake was due to the liberation of artists being free to pursue their own preoccupations and not having to depict weighty and important themes. 

This view is at variance with other conceptions of realism at the time,  which emphasised a importance of the artists social and political concerns and hence the incompatibility of realism with the notion of art-for-arts sake. Fromentin contends, more radically than Hegel, saying that 'the great Dutch school seemed to think of nothing but painting well' and that it is characterised by 'the total absence of what today we call *a subject*'. Here, *subject* does not mean the depicted content, but the moral, emotional, and intellectual message that it is intended to convey. He is contrasting the weighty and worthy requirements of academic history painting to the seemingly inconsequential incidents and events depicted in many Dutch paintings.

Rather than looking for deeper meaning, [[Eugune Fromentin]] feels we should recognise the paintings as art first, and that the quality of a painting is therefore independent of subject.

> What motive had a Dutch painter in painting a picture? None. And notice that he never asked for one. A peasant with a drunken red nose looks at you with his heavy eye and laughs with open mouth showing his teeth, raising a jug; if it is well painted, it has its value.

It is not clear what painting Fromentin is referring to here, but we can consider it alongside [[Adriaen van Ostade]].  Ostade specialised in the depcition of peasants drinking, merry-making and fighting, both out of doors and in dimly lit interiors. Despite a slight tendency towards [[caricature]] of the figures, his work is characterised by subtle [[chiaroscuro]], and a delicate use of colour. His work, and works similar raise the question; who is the work intended for? One possibility is that the intensive urbanisation of Dutch society created a market for images of rural life, showcasing 'imagined freedoms' permitted outside a tightly regulated civic environment. However, it also seems likely that the work is intended to reinforce the self-perception of city dwellers as more cultivated and sophisticated than rural folk.
